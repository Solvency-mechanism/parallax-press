What it is

Externalization describes costs or benefits of an activity falling on people outside the transaction that produces them. In environmental economics, a negative externality exists when a production or consumption decision imposes harm on others that is not fully reflected in the decision-maker's private incentives. Air pollution affecting neighbors is an example; a positive externality benefits others. EPA, Economic Theory, section A.2.

A cost passed to a customer through an agreed price is not automatically an externality. Nor does unequal income, by itself, establish one. The useful questions are which activity creates a burden, who bears it, and which costs enter the actor's decisions. Deliberate intent is a separate factual question.

Why it matters

An output figure records the value of what is sold. It does not, on its own, measure the health, time, or environmental burdens borne elsewhere. For the San Joaquin Valley, asking about externalized costs connects agricultural production to drinking-water contamination, household spending, and public responses without assuming every harm has the same source.

Costs also differ from payments. Treatment uses real resources; a grant reallocates who finances them. Adding a grant to the treatment expense it pays can count the same resources twice. A complete account must distinguish resource costs, financing, avoided harm, and distribution. EPA, Guidelines, chapters 8 and 10.

How it works in practice

A March 2011 Pacific Institute study interviewed 37 households in four San Joaquin Valley community water systems with nitrate violations. It documented households purchasing alternative water or using filters while also paying for public water service. Recruitment used convenience sampling in three communities and exhaustive sampling in one system: this is a bounded historical example, not a current Valley-wide cost estimate. Report summary, pp. 5-6.

Responses can move costs back toward responsible activities. Under the Central Valley Water Board's Nitrate Control Program, designated dischargers, including growers, dairies, industrial facilities, and wastewater plants, must arrange testing and replacement drinking water when specified nitrate conditions are met. The Board reported replacement water reaching more than 1,200 households in designated areas in January 2024. That agency-reported milestone does not establish complete remediation or universal access. Water Board announcement.

What they say vs. what's true

Common claims and the record against them.
What they sayWhat's true

The Water Board describes CV-SALTS as collaboration that brings replacement water to nitrate-affected households. Water Board, 2024.

That description identifies a real response, not merely a promise. Replacement supply can relieve a household burden without removing contamination from an aquifer. Its existence also means an account in which producers pay nothing is too simple. The remaining task is to establish who funds each response, which households it reaches, and what health, time, and financial burdens remain. Recognition, funded assistance, and safe service are related but distinct observations.

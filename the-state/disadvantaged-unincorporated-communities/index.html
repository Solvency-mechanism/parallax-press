What it is

Disadvantaged unincorporated communities (DUCs) are inhabited, low-income communities outside city boundaries. California's SB 244, enacted in 2011, established definitions and planning requirements intended to address their infrastructure needs. Its general-plan definition uses a median household income of 80 percent or less of the statewide median; the provisions governing Local Agency Formation Commissions use a related statutory definition. The applicable definition matters when interpreting a map or eligibility decision. SB 244, sections 3 and 7.

Unincorporated does not mean ungoverned. County government, special districts, and other providers can deliver services outside cities. A community's incorporation status alone does not establish whether its water is safe, whether it has sewers, or how effectively residents influence decisions.

Why it matters

When enacting SB 244, the Legislature identified underinvestment and infrastructure deficiencies affecting many DUCs, including drinking water, drainage, sidewalks, and sewer service. It recognized legal, financial, and political barriers to investment. These are documented institutional problems; explaining them does not require assuming that every community has the same history. SB 244, legislative findings.

For the CKP, the political-economy question is how boundaries, financing, and decision-making distribute access to essential services. A household can participate fully in the surrounding economy while depending on infrastructure with limited resources. Establishing why a particular community lacks adequate service requires identifying its providers, revenue arrangements, investment history, and available remedies.

How it works in practice

Kings County's 2035 General Plan illustrates different arrangements within one county. It describes Armona, Home Garden, Kettleman City, and Stratford as unincorporated communities receiving municipal-type services through community services or public utilities districts. It distinguishes smaller settlements lacking those district arrangements. These examples contradict the claim that being outside a city automatically excludes municipal-type services. Kings County, Land Use Element, printed pages LU-4 and LU-14.

SB 244 links DUC needs to general plans, municipal service reviews, and spheres of influence: the boundaries used to plan agencies' probable future service areas. Its planning requirements create opportunities to identify deficiencies and assess financing. Identification does not itself construct a project.

State implementation guidance recommends combining information sources, mapping infrastructure, and consulting affected utilities and special districts. It cautions that Census Designated Place data alone may not adequately identify the communities the law is intended to serve. OPR, SB 244 Technical Advisory, 2013.

What they say vs. what's true

Common claims and the record against them.
What they sayWhat's true

State planning guidance presents SB 244 as a means of bringing overlooked communities and infrastructure needs into local planning. OPR, 2013.

That is a meaningful institutional change, but recognition and completed service are different outcomes. The relevant questions are whether a community is identified accurately, whether a feasible solution receives funding, and whether residents gain reliable service and effective participation. Neither automatic exclusion nor automatic improvement follows from the DUC label.

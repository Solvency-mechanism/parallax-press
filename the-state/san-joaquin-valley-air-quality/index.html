What it is

Air quality describes concentrations of pollutants in outdoor air. Important San Joaquin Valley pollutants include ground-level ozone, a gas formed through reactions involving nitrogen oxides and volatile organic compounds in sunlight, and particulate matter. PM10 includes inhalable particles generally 10 micrometers and smaller; PM2.5 is the finer fraction, generally 2.5 micrometers and smaller. EPA ozone basics, particle basics.

EPA's Green Book, with data current September 30, 2026, lists the Valley as Extreme nonattainment for the 2015 eight-hour ozone standard and Serious nonattainment for the 2012 annual PM2.5 standard. These are classifications under particular federal standards, not a national ranking or today's neighborhood reading. Ozone listing, PM2.5 listing.

Why it matters

Ozone can harm respiratory health, particularly for people with asthma. Fine particles can penetrate deeply into the lungs. These hazards warrant attention without claiming that every illness has an identifiable pollution source. EPA ozone basics, particle basics.

For the CKP, Externalization asks who bears the consequences and who pays to reduce them. Answering that requires more than establishing polluted air: exposure, attributable illness, household spending, public expenditure, and producer responsibility each need evidence.

How it works in practice

The Valley combines agricultural operations with freight traffic, other vehicles, industry, residential combustion, and wildfire smoke. Pollution includes directly emitted particles and particles formed through atmospheric chemistry. Agriculture is part of this system; it cannot be assigned the whole regional burden. The District's 2024 report describes controls addressing agricultural dust and burning, wood smoke, industrial sources, and vehicles. District annual report.

An emissions inventory measures material released by sources. An ambient monitor measures concentration at a location. Weather, transport, and chemical formation connect them, so an emissions share is not automatically the same share of exposure or illness.

A 2010 study by Meng and colleagues analyzed 1,502 Valley residents reporting physician-diagnosed asthma in the 2001 California Health Interview Survey. It linked residential locations to nearby monitors. Higher PM10 and PM2.5 were associated with frequent symptoms. For emergency visits or hospitalization, the ozone estimate was an odds ratio of 1.49 per 10 parts per billion, with a 95% confidence interval of 1.05-2.11. The corresponding PM10 interval included 1, making that estimate less conclusive. Study abstract.

These are historical associations, not present-day counts or proof that a particular farm caused an individual's illness. The study does not calculate agriculture-attributable medical expenditure.

For current conditions, use the District's monitoring and real-time information or AirNow. Regional readings help describe conditions; they do not measure every person's exposure.

What they say vs. what's true

Common claims and the record against them.
What they sayWhat's true

The District's 2024 annual report emphasizes long-term improvement and progress toward federal standards.

Improvement and remaining pollution can coexist. A claim of attainment must name the pollutant, standard, averaging period, and date. Meeting an older threshold does not establish compliance with every later standard or eliminate local exposure concerns.
